Abstract

Driving safety depends on the states a policy induces, not only on the trajectory it predicts.

End-to-end driving policies have progressed rapidly on low-cost open-loop benchmarks, yet driving safety depends on the states a policy induces and the responses it elicits. Open-loop evaluation cannot reveal compounding errors, failure recovery, or interactive behavior. Existing closed-loop benchmarks restore this feedback, but CARLA-based platforms introduce an observation-domain gap, while photorealistic platforms do not organize scenario coverage around an explicit traffic-safety taxonomy. It therefore remains unclear which safety failures current policies exhibit under photorealistic closed-loop execution.

We introduce NavSafe, a benchmark that combines closed-loop execution, photorealistic observations, and taxonomy-grounded safety-critical events. Guided by the 28 outcome leaves of RoadSafe365, we screen the nuPlan test set for relevant scenarios and supplement selected uncovered taxonomy cells through 3D-asset or dynamic-actor insertion; every admitted scenario is manually verified. NuRec reconstructs each event as an editable environment, and NexusSim executes policies under an event-based, semi-reactive protocol. NavSafe reports four event-level metrics and success rates across six safety capabilities. NavSafe provides a controlled simulator stress test of closed-loop behavior, not an estimate of real-world crash risk.

Ten leaves cannot be reached by mining — seven have no nuPlan type at all (C-9 C-10 V-10 V-11 R-3 R-4 I-3) and three are too scarce to reach ten (C-7 V-8 R-2). Their 93 scenarios are constructed by scene editing; the mined leaves reach roughly 22 scenarios each.

Evaluation protocol

Four event-level metrics, six capability scores

Each scenario runs as a bounded closed-loop event whose contract specifies its trigger, goal region, safety predicates, termination order, time budget, and trace evidence. A short log-replay warm-up initializes the policy history before scoring; the policy receives only its declared sensor and navigation inputs. All metric thresholds are frozen before test evaluation.

DS

Driving Score

Event completion multiplied by declared infraction penalties, averaged over valid trials.

SR

Success Rate

1 if the event reaches its goal within budget without a disallowed infraction, else 0.

DE

Driving Efficiency

Checkpoint-wise ratio of ego speed to nearby-traffic speed; not reported when that reference is undefined.

Comf.

Comfortness

Fraction of temporal segments inside the nuPlan-derived acceleration, jerk, yaw-rate and yaw-acceleration bounds.

DSi = ECi · ∏e∈Ei pe    DS = (100 / Nv) · Σi∈V DSi    with ECi ∈ [0,1], pe ∈ [0,1]
ASRk = 100 · Σi∈Vk SRi / |Vk|,  k ∈ {LF, RC, RWN, TCC, VRU, IDTA}    Vk = Ik ∩ V
reported together with each denominator |Vk|, the macro-average ASR, and the worst-capability score ASRmin

ASR depends only on binary success — progress, efficiency, comfort and partial penalties cannot increase it. Each event is assigned to one primary capability before policy evaluation.

Research questions

What the benchmark is asked to answer

RQ1

What does NavSafe expose beyond open-loop evaluation?

Terminal reasons — ego-attributable collision, roadway departure, rule violation, deadlock, timeout, runtime failure, reconstruction invalidity — reported by leaf, capability and provenance; paired e⁰/e⁺ failure flips isolate sensitivity to the inserted factor. RQ1 also audits the benchmark itself: rejection flow, capability-mapping agreement, scorer audit precision/recall, oracle solvability and reconstruction-invalid rates.

RQ2

Does post-training narrow the OL–CL gap?

ReCogDrive and MTDrive provide the controlled contrasts: architecture and scale are fixed within each released pair, and per-seed RLFT-minus-SFT changes are reported with 95% intervals. Improvements in SR or ASR support safety transfer; improvements confined to DS, DE or Comf. indicate better quality or conservatism, not safer capability.

Scope and limitations

NavSafe provides validity evidence within the declared simulator, not proof of a real-world safety construct. It over-samples rare and constructed hazards and has no exposure model, so its score is neither a crash-rate estimate nor an on-road safety certification.

The simulation-to-reality gap remains the principal limitation. Photorealistic rendering does not guarantee correct sensor noise, geometry, contact physics or human behavior; novel viewpoints can expose reconstruction artifacts, inserted assets can mismatch illumination, and scripted actors can react unlike people. Quality envelopes, paired references, provenance stratification, replay ablations and sensitivity analyses reduce this ambiguity but cannot remove it.

Dashed leaves are unpopulated by log mining — their episodes must be generated (C-9 C-10 V-10 V-11 R-3 R-4 I-3, and C-8 is context-only: on_carpark supplies the setting, not the event). Source: docs/research/Navsafe_taxonomy_short.md §§1–4, §8.2.

Counts are scenarios, not frames: a type hit on several frames of one scene counts once, and a scene can hold several types — so the numbers overlap and never sum to 421. That is why leaves and capabilities report how many of their types occur rather than a scene total. Under s44, C-7 Head-On and V-8 Illegal U-Turn are mined leaves whose every type has zero navhard scenes. Statistics source: docs/navhard_scenario_stats.md (computed 2026-08-04) from navhard_md_logs descriptors + the nuPlan scenario_tag table.

Scene-Editing Pipeline — Design Plan

How the 83 actor-built scenarios that mining cannot supply get built: what the pipeline produces, what a human verifies, and what to implement in which order.

How to read this Part I is the design. Part II is the per-leaf build spec — which assets, where they go, how they move, and what a human confirms. Part III is what to build, in order.

Part I

The design

What the pipeline is responsible for, what it deliberately is not, and the one artifact it produces — a recipe precise enough to rebuild an identical scene.

1 What this pipeline is for

NavSafe measures coverage in taxonomy leaves: ≥ 10 scenarios per leaf across 28 leaves, 500 in total. The 73 nuPlan scenario types are the mining vocabulary — the tags through which a leaf gets populated.

Most of the 500 come from mining navhard / navtest / test. Of the ten leaves mining cannot serve, nine are built here — 9 of the 28 leaves, 83 scenarios. The tenth, C-9 Single-Vehicle, has no actors at all — it is defined entirely by ground material and HDRI, which this pipeline does not touch (§2 below). It is out of scope here and reported unpopulated.

generated    83   over 9 leaves                                   (Part II)
mined       407    over the remaining 18 leaves, ≥ 10 each → ~22 each
unpopulated  10    C-9 — out of scope for this pipeline
──────────────────
total       500

The output is not a scenario — it is a recipe for a scenario, precise enough that anyone can rebuild the identical scene later.

2 What the pipeline does

Three things:

  • Fetch the asset the recipe names — from UrbanVerse or AssetHarvester, converted to a 3DGS PLY the NuRec server can load.
  • Place it and move it as the recipe specifies — a fixed pose, or a per-frame trajectory.
  • Build and freeze the recipe — resolve the authored intent into absolute numbers and write an immutable file.

Everything else is out of scope: the mining sweep, host-scene selection, reconstruction, running the policy, tracing, scoring, coverage tables.

3 What the pipeline does not judge

The pipeline does not decide whether a built scenario is correct, plausible or faithful to its leaf. A human does, from a rendered episode. So there is no automated check for visibility, scale, orientation, grounding, appearance harmony, or whether the event reads as its intended leaf — those are all perceptual judgments, and Part II gives the reviewer a checklist per leaf instead.

The one thing code does verify is file integrity: a sha256 per baked pose array, so a corrupted or hand-edited recipe fails loudly rather than quietly replaying something else.

4 What a recipe pins

Injected actors are non-reactive, so an actor's entire trajectory is knowable before any policy runs. The recipe is therefore fully baked — per-frame absolute poses, with no controller, parameters, random seed or solver at use time.

Same recipe → the same scenario, independent of the policy under test.

identitywhich asset (source uid + sha256), which host scene, which world_version and recon artifact
geometryper-frame poses in ego frame-0 coordinates, plus the route polyline they are measured against
semanticswhich leaf this populates, and the reviewer checklist that decides whether it does
integritysha256 of every baked pose array

5 The four edit ops

All four already exist and are render/sim-consistent — the render mirrors the sim state, with no render-only or score-only objects.

opwhat it does
insertnew object at a fixed pose
replacedelete an agent, draw an asset on its trajectory
relocatere-pose an existing actor on a synthetic path, optionally swapping the asset
removedrop non-ego agents

Prefer relocate over insert where a suitable actor exists. With no archetype swap it keeps the actor's baked appearance — a pure per-frame pose override — which is the most reliable path, because the server can silently drop a freshly inserted asset.

6 Event timing under non-reactive actors

A non-reactive actor's timing relative to the ego depends on how fast the policy drives. A car baked to cross at t = 3.2 s is missed entirely by a slow policy and struck head-on by a fast one.

The remedy is placement discipline: put the scenario-defining event a short arc after the replay hand-off, before ego divergence accumulates, and record the ego-arrival window the recipe assumes so a mistimed episode is detectable. This bites hardest on V-10 and C-7, where the whole test is a timed gap.

7 Trajectory: authoring, storage, and how eval applies it

The native format

A trajectory is a per-frame state array on a track — the simulator's own representation, with T = the host's scenario frame count:

track["state"] = {
    "position": (T, 3) float32,   # x, y, z
    "heading":  (T,)   float32,   # rad
    "velocity": (T, 2) float32,   # vx, vy, world frame
    "valid":    (T,)   bool,      # does the actor exist this frame
}

Producing those four arrays is the whole job. Everything below is either how they are authored or how they are read.

Authoring: route-frame parameters

A trajectory is authored as (reference polyline, template, parameters) — arc length s along the reference, lateral offset, heading offset, speed — because that is how a human states the intent ("52 m ahead, in my lane, facing me, 8 m/s"). World coordinates are the output, never the input.

The reference polyline is one of: the ego's logged route, a lane centreline from the map, or an inline polyline. The generator already exists (nexussim/scenario/edits.py::relocate_agent) with five modes:

modemotionkey parameters
staticfixed pointarc, lateral, yaw_offset_deg
dynamicconstant speed along the reference+ speed
brakingconstant speed, then decelerate to a stop+ deceleration_mps2, maneuver_start_frame
cut_inadvances while interpolating lateral offset+ start_lateral, end_lateral, maneuver_duration_frames
crossingtraverses laterally, reaching the reference centre at conflict_frame+ conflict_frame

Two properties worth knowing. Heading follows the velocity vector when the actor is moving, so a crossing actor faces where it is going rather than along the reference tangent. And align_to_ego_at_conflict re-anchors the start arc on where the ego is at conflict_frame — this is how a timing intent ("arrive as the ego reaches the conflict point") is solved into geometry, and it is why the recipe records assumed_ego_arrival_window_s: under a non-reactive design that solve needs an assumed ego arrival, taken from the log-replay ego.

Three extensions the nine leaves need:

  • Switchable reference polyline. Everything is currently anchored on the ego's logged route, and crossing approximates a cross-street approach as a lateral slide across it. V-10's mainline platoon must run on the mainline lane, R-2's cyclist on a cross street, R-3's delivery robot on a sidewalk.
  • Group parameters. A platoon with one usable gap (V-10), a cyclist group (R-2) — one spec per track cannot express a shared gap structure.
  • An acceleration profile. A standing-start dart (R-4); dynamic is constant speed.

Storage: bake the output, inline it

Store the arrays, not the parameters. The generator depends on the host's sampled route, on T, on dt_s and on the hand-off frame; freezing parameters would let any of those silently change the trajectory later.

At navhard's T = 44 an actor's four arrays are ~1.2 KB, so they are inlined in the recipe — one file, no companion-file drift, and the recipe's own checksum covers everything. A sidecar .npz plus sha256 is the fallback for long episodes.

  • All four arrays are baked, not just position. velocity because TTC, BEV and the agent observations read it, and recomputing it from finite differences at replay would give different numbers. valid because an actor need not exist at every frame.
  • Static objects store one pose plus static: true; the loader expands it.
  • Stored alongside: T, dt_s, frame_timestamps_us (so a host mismatch is detectable), after_frame (the arc-0 anchor), keep_appearance (decides the z convention), and the authored parameters plus reference polyline as documentation — diffable and re-authorable, but never re-executed at replay.

Applying at eval

A baked trajectory is indistinguishable from a logged one — both are track["state"] arrays indexed by scenario_timestep. So it rides the existing log-replay path:

recipe.yaml
  │  replay.py — verify checksum, build the edit spec
  ▼
env_cfg.scenario_edits = [{"tool": "apply_baked_track", ...}]
  │  eval_py123d.py switches to NexusSimEditEnv when edits are present
  ▼  (the stock env stays pristine)
apply_scenario_edits(sd, edits)          — once at load, idempotent via a metadata marker
  ▼
sd["tracks"][id]["state"] = {position, heading, velocity, valid}
  │  per step
  ▼
LogReplayTraffic.step → agent_manager.update_agents(timestep=env.scenario_timestep)
  │
  ├──▶ agent_states → BEV · collision · TTC · scoring
  └──▶ renderer mirror (_build_dynamic_objects) → NuRec per-frame pose

env.scenario_timestep is the array index: episode frame k reads element k. Both consumers read the same number, which is why the render mirrors the sim state.

One new edit tool is needed — apply_baked_track, which writes the arrays into the track instead of computing them, registered alongside the existing tools in EDIT_TOOLS. The env, traffic manager, renderer and scoring are unchanged, because what they see is an ordinary logged track.

Three conventions the recipe must pin

  • Arrays index scenario frames, not frames since the hand-off. scenario_timestep starts at 0 and ego_replay_frames is a frame number on the same axis, so arrays must cover 0 … T−1 including the pre-hand-off replay stretch. arc parameters, meanwhile, are measured from the hand-off as arc 0 — two different origins, and both are recorded.
  • valid = False means moved off-screen, not deleted. The server has no delete flag, so the renderer relocates absent actors out of view. Where that off-screen pose lands can still be visible from another camera, which makes it a reviewer check rather than a guarantee.
  • The z convention depends on keep_appearance. Kept-appearance actors are ground-clamped by the renderer, so their z stays at road z; swapped-in assets are base-origin and have their base pinned to road z. Recording which one applies is what keeps the object from floating or sinking on rebuild.

Part II

The nine leaves

6 leaves have no nuPlan type — 10 scenarios each, built from nothing: C-10 · V-10 · V-11 · R-3 · R-4 · I-3. 3 leaves are too scarce to reach 10 — the deficit is built and the real scenarios are kept: C-7 (1 mined → 9) · V-8 (1 mined → 9) · R-2 (5 mined → 5). A seventh no-nuPlan-type leaf, C-9 Single-Vehicle, is out of scope — this pipeline only inserts and moves actors, and C-9 is defined entirely by ground material and HDRI (§1).

Each entry gives asset · placement · trajectory · reviewer checklist. Placement and trajectory are stated as authoring intent; §7 covers how that becomes baked arrays and how eval applies them.

Applies to every leaf — not repeated below
  • the asset renders at all, at the right scale, upright, and sitting on the road
  • an actor with valid = False frames is parked off-screen, not deleted — confirm that off-screen pose is not visible from another camera
  • the event falls inside the recipe's assumed_ego_arrival_window_s, so the policy actually meets it (§6)

replay.py is the surface a benchmark user touches: recipe in, scenario_edits out, fed to the existing eval path, with checksums verified first. The baked state arrays enter through apply_baked_track, so the env, traffic manager, renderer and scoring are unchanged.

Review cards

Since verification is human, the pipeline's job is to make review fast: per candidate, render cam_f0.gif and topdown.gif, print the placement numbers a reviewer needs to sanity-check (lane width, closing time, spawn arc), show the e⁰/e⁺ pair side by side, and attach that leaf's checklist from Part II. The reviewer records a verdict plus a reason code; reason codes aggregate into template fixes rather than per-episode patches.

At 83 scenarios and a few seconds of video each, one review pass is a few hours and parallelises across people.
